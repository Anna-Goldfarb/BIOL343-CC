Part 1 — Import and inspect

The data file is data/oats.csv: the yield of oats in a split-plot field trial. The field was divided into six blocks; each block was divided into three subplots, each sown with one of three oat varieties; and each subplot was split into four strips that received four rates of nitrogen fertilizer, measured on each strip. The subplots are labelled a, b and c within each block. One row is one strip. The instructions describe the trial and the variables. Do not edit the file by hand.

So far every model has treated each row as an independent observation. This week the rows come in groups within groups, and you will fit models that know it.

As in earlier challenges, the tasks tell you what to produce, not which function to use. The starter lines in some chunks are commented out so that the blank template knits; remove the # as you fill them in.

Question 1: Which column is the response? For each of the other columns, say whether it is continuous or categorical, and whether it should be a fixed or a random effect, and why. Variety was assigned to subplots and nitrogen to strips: how many independent replicates does the trial have for comparing varieties (explain your answer)?

Answer: The yeild column is the response. The nitrogen column is continuous and should be a fixed effect because we are interested in the amount of nitrogen impact. The varienty column is categorical and should be a fixed effect because we want compare yield among specific varieties. The block column is categorical and should be a random effect because it accounts for environmental variation. The subplot column is categorical and should be a random effect because it accounts for instances of non-independence. There are six independent replicates per variety (18 subplots in total) because each variety was assigned to one subplot within each of the six blocks.

Question 2: Compare the variety estimates, their standard errors and the residual degrees of freedom between the two models. Which model is pseudoreplicated for variety, and why? Which standard errors would you trust more?

Answer: Variety estimates are similar, but standard error increases and residual degrees of freedom decreases in the subplot-level model. The strip-level model is pseudoreplicated for variety because it treats 24 strips per variety as independent replicates, when there are only six independently assigned subplots per variety. I would trust the standard errors of the subplot-level model more because they reflect the true level of replication for variety, although neither model accounts for the non-independence of subplots within blocks.

Question 3: What share of the variation in yield does each variance component hold? Do the three varieties differ in yield? How do the standard errors of nitrogen and of variety compare with the two linear models in Part 2, and why?

Answer: Blocks account for 42.54% of the variance, subplots nested within blocks account for 23.87%, and residual variation accounts for 33.59%. Therefore, 66.41% of the variance remaining after accounting for fixed effects is attributable to the nested spatial structure of the experiment. There is no statistically significant evidence that yield differs among the three varieties, as the likelihood ratio test showed that including variety did not significantly improve model fit (χ² = 3.203, df = 2, p = 0.2016). The standard error of nitrogen decreases in the mixed model because accounting for variation between blocks and subplots allows the effect of nitrogen within subplots to be estimated more precisely. The standard errors of variety are larger than in the strip-level model because the mixed model corrects for pseudoreplication, but smaller than in the subplot-level model because it accounts for shared block-level variation and retains strip-level information.
